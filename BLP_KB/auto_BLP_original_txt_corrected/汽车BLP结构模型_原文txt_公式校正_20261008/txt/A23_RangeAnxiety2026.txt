Panle Jia Barwick, Shanjun Li, and Tianli Xia
NBER Working Paper No. 34871
February 2026

                         ABSTRACT

Range anxiety, the fear of depleting battery before reaching a charging station, is often cited as a
major barrier to electric vehicle (EV) adoption, yet there has been limited formal economic analysis
to quantify its magnitude and examine the policy implications. We develop a continuous-time
dynamic model of EV usage and charging decisions to micro-found range anxiety as the utility loss
from feasible yet unrealized trips due to perceived range constraints. Using high-frequency data of
188,000 EV trips and 26,000 charging events among 8,000 EVs in Shanghai, we recover model
parameters governing consumer driving and charging decisions. The estimates imply that, across
EV models with varying driving ranges, average range anxiety was about $1,900 in 2021 but
declined to $1,200 in 2024, driven by improvements in charging infrastructure and, especially,
increases  in  driving  range. Policy simulations underscore  the importance  of coordinating
investments in driving range and charging infrastructure to address range anxiety. Relative to
socially optimal levels, Shanghai’s EV market has under-invested in driving range but over-
invested in charging infrastructure.

1  Introduction

With the transportation sector responsible for nearly one-third of global carbon emissions,

transitioning to electric vehicles (EVs) powered by a clean energy grid has become a corner-

stone of global decarbonization efforts. Since being introduced into the mass market in 2011,

global passenger EV sales have grown rapidly, reaching 17.1 million units, or 20% of new
passenger vehicle sales, by 2024.1 Market penetration, however, varies widely: EVs accounted

for 40% of new vehicle sales in China, 28% in Europe, 11% in the United States, and just 5%
in the rest of the world. Even within countries or regions, adoption is highly uneven.2 Under-

standing the barriers to EV adoption and identifying solutions to address them is critical for

the next phase of EV diffusion.

   Consumer surveys and industry reports regularly identify range anxiety as one of the most

significant barriers to EV adoption (Wardlaw, 2020; Chokshi et al., 2021; Klier and Rubenstein,

2022; Rainieri et al., 2023; Bloomberg Intelligence, 2024). Range anxiety refers to the fear of

depleting the battery before reaching a charging station, stemming from the combination of
limited driving range and inadequate charging infrastructure.3 Despite the widely recognized

importance of range anxiety, formal analysis of the phenomenon and the cost-effectiveness of

strategies to mitigate it remains limited. To bridge this gap, we develop a micro-foundation for

range anxiety utilizing a continuous-time dynamic model of consumer EV usage and charging

behavior. Based on estimated parameters, we quantify range anxiety in monetary terms and

perform policy simulations to evaluate the relative efficiency of increasing vehicle driving range

versus expanding charging infrastructure.

   In the model, trip needs of varying distances arrive stochastically, and EV drivers decide

whether to undertake a trip or initiate charging based on their remaining battery range, access

to charging network, and charging prices. We define range anxiety as the utility loss from

feasible but forgone trips, arising from the driver’s perceived risk of battery depletion before

reaching a charging station. Our empirical analysis leverages a unique, granular, and high-

frequency dataset on EV driving and charging behavior. It records the location and remaining

  We begin by documenting some key patterns in the data. First, the charging probability,

defined as the share of charging events among both driving and charging events, is higher

when the remaining range (i.e., state of charge or SOC) is low. This is intuitive, as a limited

remaining range both constrains the ability to take trips and increases the need to recharge.

Second, the probability of charging rises with better charging access and falls with charging

prices, the pattern becoming more pronounced at lower remaining ranges. Third, drivers with

home charging access rely primarily on home charging to meet their needs. Their probability

of using public fast charging is much less sensitive to remaining range than it is for those

without home access, highlighting charging behavior differences between the two groups.

   Empirical support for range anxiety stems from a robust pattern of “missing trips” in the

data, defined as feasible trips that are foregone when they would result in a low SOC among

BEVs. This pattern persists, though to varying degrees, across several sub-samples: daytime

vs. nighttime, commuting time vs. leisure time, weekdays vs. weekends, winter vs. summer,

and drivers with vs. without home chargers. However, a placebo test on PHEVs does not

detect this pattern. To provide causalevidence, we conduct a regression analysis examining

how the projected remaining range (after a trip) affects the hazard rate of trip-taking, and

how this effect varies with charging density or access. To address potential endogeneity in

charging access, we construct a shift-share instrument by interacting the pre-sample (2018)

number of points of interest (POIs) with the declining cost of charging equipment over time.

Our analysis shows that drivers forgo over 60% of trips that would reduce their remaining

range below 50 km and approximately 14% of trips that would leave them between 50-100

km. Notably, improved charging access mitigates this behavior: the expansion of the charging

network observed from 2021 to 2024 reduces the frequency of missing trips by nearly one-

quarter.

   Guided by these data patterns, we develop and estimate a continuous-time dynamic model

to recover the structural parameters governing driving and charging decisions. We incorporate

rich consumer heterogeneity and state variables.  Consumers are characterized by the full

driving range of their EV (e.g., 250km vs. 400km), the charging network structure (2021 vs.

2024), and whether they have access to a home charger. The set of state variables for a driver

at any given point in time includes the remaining range of his vehicle, attributes of his current

location (number of local charging stations, local charging price, number of local amenities,

home vs. away from home), and whether at daytime or nighttime. The estimates suggest that

the probability of charging decreases with remaining range and charging prices, but increases

with better charging access and rises for vehicles with longer driving ranges. In contrast, the

probability of driving rises with remaining range but drops sharply if taking a trip reduces

the remaining range below 50 or 100 km, or if the trip ends in areas with sparse or costly

charging options.  Finally, the estimated price elasticity of charging demand at fast public

stations is -0.98 for drivers without home charging and -0.28 for those with home charging.

The lower elasticity among drivers with home charging likely reflects their infrequent use of

public stations.

   Using the estimated parameters, we find that the willingness to pay (WTP) for improve-

ments in driving range and charging access is substantial, especially among drivers without

home charging. In 2018, the average driving range of the existing EV stock was 318km, and

there wer only about 100 fast charging stations in the city. By 2024, the average range in-

creased to 383km, while the number of fast charging stations surged to 2,268 (with a total of

40,000 fast chargers). For drivers without home chargers, the estimated WTP for these im-

provements is Y62,000 (approximately $8,600), compared with Y18,000 (or $2,500) for those

with home chargers. The WTP exhibits diminishing returns for both driving range and charg-

ing access. Moreover, the two attributes are substitutes: the combined WTP from improving

both is smaller than the sum of their individual effects.

  We next quantify range anxiety by monetizing the utility loss from feasible but unrealized

trips due to fear of getting stranded on the road. The average range anxiety across vehicles

with different ranges was $2,550 in 2018, falling to $1,900 in 2021 and $1,200 in 2024. The

averages mask significant heterogeneity across EVs with different driving range. The decline

in range anxiety over time reflects both the expansion of the charging network and, especially,

improvements in driving range. Overall, the reduction in range anxiety accounts for over 50%

of WTP for improved driving range and charging access among drivers without home chargers,

and roughly 15% among those with home charging.

  We use simulations to evaluate two common strategies for alleviating range anxiety: ex-

panding charging infrastructure vs.  increasing driving range. The socially optimal level of

each is determined by maximizing the aggregate welfare, defined as consumer surplus net of

the social costs of improving range (via larger battery capacity) and charging access (through

denser station networks). Our results reveal that the actual levels of charging access and driv-

ing range in Shanghai’s EV market deviate substantially from the social optimum. Between

2018 and 2024, driving ranges consistently fell short of optimal levels. Charging infrastruc-

   Our paper contributes to four strands of literature. First, it extends the extensive work on

consumer demand for EVs. Prior studies have primarily examined barriers to EV adoption—

such as high upfront purchase costs, limited access to charging infrastructure, and the role of

government policies in addressing these challenges (Springel, 2021a; Rapson and Muehlegger,

2023; Bushnell et al., 2025). A key finding in this literature is the importance of indirect net-

work effects (the “chicken-and-egg” problem), whereby charging infrastructure investment and

EV adoption reinforce each other through positive spillovers (Li et al., 2017; Li, 2023; Springel,

2021b; Jacqz and Johnston, 2024; Wang, 2024). While range anxiety is frequently invoked

as the implicit mechanism linking charging access to EV adoption, it has remained largely

without a formal definition in the literature. Qin and Scott (2025) examine how driving range

interacts with fuel prices to influence EV demand as a proxy for range anxiety because the

disutility of limited range is magnified when lower fuel prices increase the demand for travel.

More recently, Kaps et al. (2025) illustrate the presence of range anxiety by demonstrating

that while BEVs and gasoline vehicles exhibit similar total mileage, BEVs are significantly

less likely to be used for long-distance trips (exceeding 150 km), a gap that narrows as charg-

ing network density increases. In contrast, our paper provides a micro-foundation for range

anxiety through a continuous-time dynamic model, allowing us to quantify range anxiety in a

utility maximization framework using high-frequency data on driving and charging behavior.

   Second, our paper contributes to the emerging literature on EV charging. A central ques-

tion in this literature is how charging behavior responds to prices across different settings,

including home, workplace, and public stations. Field experiments document substantial vari-

ation in price elasticities across contexts (Garg et al., 2024; La Nauze et al., 2024; Bernard
et al., 2025; Bailey et al., 2025).4 Relying on highly disaggregated data, other studies infer

EV usage from household electricity consumption in California (Burlig et al., 2021), estimate

the time cost of fueling gasoline vehicles among drivers in Michigan and use this estimate to

benchmark the cost of charging (Dorsey et al., 2025), and examine the impact of charging on

the electricity grid in Germany (Heid et al., 2024). Our paper extends this literature by exam-

a unified framework applied to highly granular observational data. Our structural estimates

indicate a price elasticity of –1 for public charging. Notably, EV drivers with home charging

exhibit a stronger response to price changes at home than to prices at public stations.

   Third, our study contributes to the literature on policy design aimed at promoting EV

adoption and infrastructure investment. We quantify the interaction between driving range

and charging access through a micro-founded model of EV driving and charging decisions.

Zhou and Li (2018) highlight the critical role of indirect network effects in EV diffusion and

argue that government intervention may be necessary to coordinate consumer adoption and

infrastructure development. Barwick et al. (2024) and Remmy (2025) examine automakers’ in-

centives in supplying product attributes such as driving range, showing that market power and

network effects can lead to socially suboptimal outcomes. Relatedly, Arkolakis et al. (2025)

analysis demonstrates that the combination of limited driving range (supplied by automakers)

and sparse charging access (provided by station investors) is the fundamental source of range

anxiety, and that this interaction jointly determines the socially optimal levels of vehicle range

and charging network size.

    Finally, our paper contributes to the literature on public-private interactions.  Li and

Springel (2025) study the relationship between public and private charging stations in the

U.S. Cabral et al. (2025) examines the substitutability and complementarity between public

and private R&D. Our policy simulations underscore the importance of coordinating policy

instruments such as subsidies that target different market participants, echoing the insight

from Liu (2025) on the value of a coordinated policy mix in the EV sector.

   The remainder of the paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 describes the data and discusses

key data patterns. Section 3 presents the empirical model and estimation strategy. Section 4

discusses the estimation results. Section 5 conducts policy simulations. Section 6 concludes.


2  Data Description and Evidence

2.1  Data Description

Background and Data Sources  Our analysis draws on data from Shanghai, a city with

24.9 million residents and 4.15 million households, spanning an area roughly the size of the


                                        5

===== [PDF 第 8 页｜印刷第 6 页] =====
state of Delaware. By the end of 2024, Shanghai had 5.1 million registered passenger vehicles,

including 1.51 million EV, the largest EV stock of any single city in the world. In 2024 alone,

278,000 new EVs were sold in Shanghai, accounting for 45% of all new passenger vehicle sales,
slightly above the national average of 40%.5 For comparison, the state of California in the

U.S. has 39 million residents and 25 million passenger cars, of which 2.2 million are EVs. In

2024, 443,000 new EVs were sold in the state, accounting for 25% of new vehicle sales.

   Our main dataset consists of eight weekly cross-sections of detailed EV usage and driving

records, with four weeks drawn from 2021 and four from 2024. The four weeks fall in January,

March, August, and October and capture seasonal variations across the year. Each cross-

section contains data on 1,100 battery EVs, randomly sampled from the active residential-use

EV fleet in Shanghai, with complete information on all trips and charging events during the

week. Trip records are based on GPS tracking and include driving location and distance, while

charging records provide the state of charge (SOC, i.e., remaining battery capacity measured

in percentage from 0% to 100%), charging duration, and price.

  We classify the continuous vehicle telematics data into three distinct event types: trips,

charging events, and parking. A trip is defined as a non-charging movement segment, begin-

ning with vehicle start and ending with shut-off. Consecutive segments separated by intervals

of less than two minutes are aggregated into a single trip.

   Charging events are classified as public or private. A public charging event is constructed

by combining the charging session with the immediately preceding and subsequent trips,
provided they occur within thirty minutes of the session’s start or end.6 Conversely, private

charging (or home charging) refers to sessions at chargers in residential areas or workplaces.

These are typically restricted-access, slow AC units that do not impose commercial markups.

Parking is defined as any period where the vehicle is turned off and not classified as a trip or

charging event.

   Applying these definitions yields a final sample of approximately 188,000 trips and 26,000

charging events. There are 8,500 commercial fast charging sessions and 17,500 private slow-

charging sessions. We exclude public slow-charging events, as they are rare and account for

less than 5% of the total. Table A1 reports summary statistics of the sample.

3  Empirical Model of EV Usage and Charging

This section provides a micro-foundation of range anxiety by developing a continuous-time

discrete-choice dynamic model to characterize EV driving and charging decisions.  Range

anxiety is captured by the utility loss from drivers’ decisions to forgo certain trips that would

leave the post-trip (ending) range low. We then translate the utility loss into monetary terms
using the util-dollar conversion implied in the model.16

  A continuous-time dynamic model has two advantages over a discrete-time approach. First,

trips and charging events occur in continuous time with irregular intervals between them. A

continuous-time model allows us to directly exploit variation in these intervals and avoids dis-

cretizing time into coarse units (e.g., hours), thereby eliminating discretization bias. Second,

when multiple charging or driving events occur within a short period, the continuous-time

framework does not require imposing an arbitrary ordering of events, since the probability

that two events occur at exactly the same instant is zero by construction.


3.1  Setup

A representative driver of type i (type defined below) operates a vehicle with a full battery
range of ¯Ri. Trip opportunities arrive according to a Poisson process. Upon arrival, the driver
decides whether to undertake the trip. Accepting a trip yields utility but alters the state of

the driver. For example, taking a trip reduces the remaining battery and relocates the vehicle

to an area with different charging accessibility and amenities.

3.3  Estimation Details

arrival and transition process based on the observed trip arrivals and the time interval between

these events. In the second step, we evaluate the empirical likelihood of observed trips and

charging events and estimate structural parameters in trip utility and charging costs. This

estimation procedure is similar to the discrete-choice dynamic model, but it leverages the time

intervals between events in a continuous manner (via the hazard rates) and avoids the need

to specify an arbitrary event ordering.

5  Quantification and Policy Simulations

We evaluate counterfactual improvements in driving range and charging infrastructure by

monetizing the model’s value function, which represents the lifetime utility of vehicle usage.

Specifically, we analyze two primary policy levers:  battery capacity and the scale of the

public charging network. Consumers’ willingness to pay (WTP) for these improvements arises

through three primary channels: (1) the facilitation of trips that were previously infeasible or

forgone, (2) a reduction in charging hassle costs, and (3) optimized charging behavior, which

reallocates charging to more cost-effective and accessible locations. Furthermore, we quantify

“range anxiety” as the monetary value of forgone but feasible trips, a metric determined

by both vehicle range and network coverage.  Finally, we evaluate optimal policy design by

integrating these welfare gains with cost data for battery and infrastructure investments.

risk-averse constraints of the charging state.

nearly half of new vehicle sales in Shanghai by 2024.


5.4  Policy Design

Our analysis of range anxiety highlights two key remedies for mitigating it and, in turn,

promoting EV adoption: increasing vehicle driving range and expanding the charging network.

While countries such as China and Norway have invested heavily in charging infrastructure, the

United States has lagged behind, and a large-scale build-out comparable to those countries




                                        32

===== [PDF 第 35 页｜印刷第 33 页] =====
is unlikely in the near term.33  In the absence of strong government support for charging

infrastructure, many industry experts argue that increasing vehicle driving range is the most

logical path for the next stage of EV adoption. Our following simulations aim to shed light on

different approaches to promoting EV adoptions via increasing consumers’ WTP. Specifically,

we solve for the optimal combination of EV driving range and charging network size from the

social planner’s perspective. We then compare this optimal design with observed levels, as

well as with the optimal outcome under a constrained scenario in which only one approach is

employed. While the analysis is conducted in the context of Shanghai and the quantitative

findings are unlikely to generalize to other regions, the underlying mechanisms and qualitative

insights should have broader applicability.


The Value of Battery Capacity  To validate the model’s empirical estimates, we perform

a heuristic comparison between the marginal economic benefit of extended driving range and
the associated costs of increasing battery capacity.34 A reasonable model should produce a

lifetime WTP for additional range that is of a comparable order of magnitude to the physical

cost of the required battery hardware.  According to Barwick et al. (2025b), battery pack

costs were approximately $155/kWh in 2021 and $115/kWh in 2024. Assuming a constant
efficiency factor of ¯E = 6 km/kWh, a 50-km range extension necessitates an additional battery
expenditure of approximately 50 × ($155/¯E) (Y9,042) in 2021 and 50 × ($115/¯E) (Y6,708) in

2024. These figures likely represent a lower bound for the total cost of range extension, as they

exclude auxiliary expenses related to structural redesign and energy management systems.

   Nevertheless, these cost estimates align closely with the WTP for a 50-km range increase

derived from our model (Figure 10).  Specifically, in 2021, assuming a baseline range of 350

consumer WTP for an EV. They are substitutes in that an arbitrarily large battery could

eliminate the need for public charging, and an extremely dense network would offset limited

range. However, there exists diminishing return to each technology as shown in Figure 10.

This relationship implies that a coordinated investment strategy is likely to be more efficient

than a singular focus on either technology. Figure A13 provides a stylized illustration of this

logic: the efficient trajectory from current infrastructure and battery levels toward the social

optimum is a balanced expansion path which necessitates simultaneous investment in both

technologies. A one-dimensional approach, by contrast would result in efficiency losses.

6  Conclusion

Although range anxiety consistently emerges as a key barrier to EV adoption in consumer

surveys, industry reports, and policy discussions, there has been limited economic analysis

quantifying its magnitude and evaluating the effectiveness of potential remedies. This study

provides, to our knowledge, the first analysis to micro-found range anxiety, defined as the

utility loss from unrealized yet feasible trips due to fear of battery depletion before reaching a

charging station. We operationalize this concept by developing and estimating a continuous-

time dynamic model of EV driving and charging behavior.

   Leveraging high-frequency data from 8,000 EVs in Shanghai, we identify compelling evi-

dence of range anxiety by documenting a robust pattern of “missing trips”, feasible but foregone

trips when such trips will result in low remaining range after travel. This behavioral distortion

is more pronounced in regions with sparse charging infrastructure from IV regressions. But

such a pattern does not exist for PHEVs which have auxiliary gasoline engines, further allevi-

ating the concerns for unobserved factors. Our structural analysis estimates the average range

anxiety to be approximately $1,900 per vehicle in 2021 (about 10% of the average EV purchase

price), declining to $1,200 (6% of the average price) by 2024. These aggregate figures, however,

mask substantial heterogeneity across EV models with varying driving range. The observed

37% reduction in average range anxiety over this period is primarily attributable to the rapid

expansion of the charging network (from 400 to 2,268 stations) and, more significantly, to the

concurrent improvement in average driving range from 353 km to 388 km.

   Our simulations highlight the crucial roles of two approaches in addressing range anxiety:

expanding driving range (through increased battery capacity) and enhancing charging access

(through larger charging networks). Because each approach exhibits diminishing returns, the

optimal policy requires coordinated investment in both charging infrastructure and battery

capacity. The socially optimal levels of charging network size and driving range evolve as the

costs of charging station construction and battery production decline over time. Our analysis

indicates that the realized investment trajectory diverges significantly from the optimal path:

by 2024, Shanghai has over-invested in charging infrastructure while under-investing in driving

range relative to the social optimum.

  We note two important caveats and directions for future research.  First, our data and

analysis are restricted to travel within Shanghai, and thus our estimates of range anxiety

likely represent a lower bound, as range anxiety may be more pronounced for long-distance

trips. That said, long-distance driving is less common in China than in countries such as the

U.S., partly due to China’s extensive high-speed rail system. Second, our empirical analysis


                                        36

===== [PDF 第 39 页｜印刷第 37 页] =====
is based on data from Shanghai, and the quantitative findings should be interpreted in that

context. As detailed EV usage and charging data become available elsewhere, our framework

can be adapted to quantify range anxiety and its policy implications across different settings,

where underlying preferences may vary. Another important direction for future research is to

leverage granular data to examine the optimal spatial distribution of charging infrastructure

while explicitly incorporating range anxiety in the model.

A  Appendix A. Derivation of Bellman Equation (10)

We begin by discretizing time into intervals of length ∆t. Within each interval, four events

may occur:

    1. Flow utility: The agent receives instantaneous flow utility u(s) ∆t from remaining in

state s during the interval.
    2. Travel transition: With probability qs,s′∆t, the state transitions to s′ ̸= s due to a travel
event. Upon such a transition, the agent chooses between traveling (j = 1) and not traveling

(j = 0), which yields the choice-specific values

                        ψ(1, s, s′) + V (s′) + εt,1  or  V (s) + εt,0.
