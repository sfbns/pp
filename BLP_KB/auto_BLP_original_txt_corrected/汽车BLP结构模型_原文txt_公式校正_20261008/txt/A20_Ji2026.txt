Quantifying the Welfare Effects of Electric
Vehicle Subsidies: Evidence from China


Qing Ji, Chunan Wang, Xiaoyong Zheng, Ying Fan



Abstract: We evaluate the welfare effects of China’s electric vehicle (EV) subsidy pol-
icy from 2015 to 2019. Our ﬁndings indicate that the subsidies primarily transfer
funds from the government to consumers, with car manufacturers capturing only a
small share. This outcome is driven by EV demand curvatures exceeding one and sig-
niﬁcant heterogeneity in consumer price sensitivities. We further decompose the wel-
fare effects into two key components: the baseline subsidy based on driving range and
the subsidy adjustment coefﬁcients targeting technological innovations. While the latter
intensiﬁes competition among EVs, reducing manufacturer proﬁts, it also enhances en-
vironmental beneﬁts by lowering emissions. Finally, our results underscore the need to
account for nonenvironmental externalities—such as trafﬁc accidents and congestion—
as well as the assumptions regarding the transportation mode underlying the outside
choice in welfare analysis. Varying these assumptions can yield opposing conclusions
on the overall social welfare effect.

JEL Codes: H23, Q51, Q58

Keywords: electric vehicles (EVs), subsidy, social welfare

the problem of air pollution caused by motor vehicles. However, EVs also face challenges,

side, we use a Bertrand–Nash oligopoly model for differentiated products to estimate

  A unique feature of the EV subsidies in China is the two-step process used to de-
termine subsidy amounts since 2017. First, a baseline subsidy is calculated based on the
vehicle’s driving range, similar to the approach in other countries (e.g., the United
States calculates subsidies based on battery capacity; Sheldon and Dua 2019). In the
second step, two adjustment coefﬁcients are applied based on the vehicle’s battery en-
ergy density and energy efﬁciency (electricity consumption per 100 kilometers). The
ﬁnal subsidy is the product of the baseline amount and the two adjustment coefﬁcients.
These coefﬁcients were introduced to encourage technological innovation, aiming to
make the EV ﬂeet more energy efﬁcient. To our knowledge, no other country has im-
plemented a similar policy. In our welfare analysis, we decompose the effects of the sub-
sidy into two components: one for the baseline subsidy and another for the adjustment
coefﬁcients. This enables us to compare the welfare effects of both components.
  Our results show that the subsidy policy primarily transfers funds from the govern-
ment to consumers, with car manufacturers receiving a small share. The subsidies lead
to a signiﬁcant increase in consumer welfare, a modest rise in manufacturer proﬁts, and
a substantial decrease in government revenue. This outcome is driven by the demand
curvatures for EVs being larger than one and signiﬁcant heterogeneity in price sensitiv-
ities among consumers, prompting manufacturers to engage in intense competition and
lower prices, effectively passing on most of the subsidies to price-sensitive consumers.
  The subsidies also enhance social welfare by reducing greenhouse gas (CO2) and air
pollutant (PM2.5, SO2, NOx) emissions as consumers shift from ICEVs to EVs. How-
ever, the welfare impact of the subsidies on trafﬁc congestion and accidents depends on
the assumed transportation mode when consumers do not purchase a new vehicle (the
outside choice). When the outside choice is assumed to be biking or walking, the sub-
sidies result in a welfare loss, as some consumers are incentivized to switch from biking/
walking to purchasing a vehicle, leading to increased trafﬁc congestion and accidents.
When the outside choice is assumed to be continuing to drive a ﬁve-year-old ICEV,
the subsidies create a welfare gain, as they encourage some consumers to switch from
an older ICEV to a new EV. Since, on average, ICEVs are driven 1.43 times more an-
nually than EVs, this shift reduces overall trafﬁc congestion and accidents.
   Overall, the total welfare effect of the subsidies offered in the second half of 2019
ranges from a loss of 4,000.57 million RMB (0.28% of total vehicle sales) to a gain of
3,373.71 million RMB (0.24% of total vehicle sales), depending on the assumption re-
garding the outside choice. Furthermore, our results show that trafﬁc-related external-
ities are a signiﬁcant component of the overall welfare impact. Ignoring them would alter
both the sign and magnitude of the total social welfare effect. These ﬁndings emphasize
the need to carefully consider different assumptions about the outside choice and account

for nonenvironmental externalities in welfare analysis, as different assumptions can lead

  When decomposing the welfare effects into two components—the baseline subsidy
and the adjustment coefﬁcients targeting technological innovations—we ﬁnd that the
adjustment coefﬁcients have a negative impact on manufacturer proﬁts by intensifying
competition among EVs. However, they have a positive impact on environmental exter-
nalities, reducing greenhouse gas and air pollutant emissions. These ﬁndings contrast
with the effects of the baseline subsidy, demonstrating that the adjustment coefﬁcients,
designed to encourage technological innovation, have a beneﬁcial marginal effect on en-
vironmental protection.
  Our study contributes to the small but growing literature on welfare analysis of EV
subsidy policies. The closest paper to ours is Guo and Xiao (2023), which examines the
welfare effects of EV subsidy policies in Shanghai during the second half of 2019. Our
study differs from theirs in four key aspects. First, while their study quantiﬁes the wel-
fare effects of overall subsidy levels, we separately analyze the effects of the baseline sub-
sidy (based on driving range) and the adjustment coefﬁcients targeting technological
improvements. Second, their study excludes externality costs related to trafﬁc conges-
tion and accidents, focusing only on environmental externalities such as greenhouse gas
and air pollutant emissions. In contrast, we include trafﬁc-related externalities and ﬁnd
that these costs are a signiﬁcant part of the overall welfare impact; ignoring them could
lead to vastly different conclusions.
   Third, their analysis is limited to Shanghai, which has a unique quota policy. This
results in their ﬁnding that an increase in EV subsidies raises domestic ICEV prices
due to additional demand for vehicle registration, increasing quota costs for all vehi-
cles. Our analysis, conducted at the national level, is not inﬂuenced by such city-
speciﬁc policies and offers broader, more representative results. Finally, using demand
curvature analysis (Miravete et al. 2023, 2024), we show that the overshifting of sub-
sidies—a key ﬁnding in both our study and Guo and Xiao’s (2023)—is driven by de-
mand curvatures for EVs greater than one, coupled with signiﬁcant heterogeneity in
price sensitivities among consumers purchasing new cars.
   Another closely related paper is Yang and Tang (2019), which examines the effects of
early EV subsidy policies in China (2011–12) by quantifying the welfare impacts on con-
sumer welfare, ﬁrm proﬁts, government revenue, and environmental externalities. Our
study differs from theirs in two key aspects. First, we analyze the subsidy policy post-
2017, when adjustment coefﬁcients targeting technological innovations were introduced,
while their study focuses on the period before 2017, when subsidies were based solely on
driving range. Second, regarding externality effects, Yang and Tang (2019) consider only
environmental externalities in terms of greenhouse gas emissions, while we also include
the environmental externalities of air pollutants and the nonenvironmental externalities
of trafﬁc accidents and congestion. We ﬁnd that incorporating nonenvironmental exter-
nalities is crucial, as ignoring them could lead to entirely different conclusions.

  The third closely related paper is Clinton and Steinberg (2019). They ﬁrst estimate
the impact of subsidies on EV adoption and then, assuming a linear demand and supply
model, combine their results with those of Holland et al. (2016) to measure the welfare
effects of EV subsidies. Their ﬁndings suggest that EV subsidies are not welfare-
improving when considering only the beneﬁts from avoided emissions. Our study dif-
fers from theirs in two signiﬁcant ways. First, we estimate a nonlinear structural
demand and supply model using observed market share data, whereas they assume a
linear demand and supply model without estimation. As a result, our welfare analysis
is more consistent with real-world data. Second, while they focus solely on environ-
mental externalities, we incorporate both environmental and nonenvironmental exter-
nalities in our analysis.
  Our study makes two primary contributions to the existing literature. First, we
conduct a more comprehensive welfare analysis of the EV subsidy policy than previous
studies. Speciﬁcally, our analysis includes six components: consumer welfare, producer
proﬁts, government revenue, externalities related to greenhouse gas emissions, air pol-
lutant emissions, and trafﬁc accidents and congestion. Second, leveraging the unique
design of China’s subsidy policy, we decompose the welfare effects into two parts: one
related to the baseline subsidy based on driving range and the other to the adjustment
coefﬁcients targeting technological innovations. To the best of our knowledge, this ap-
proach has not been explored in the literature.
   Additionally, our study contributes to the broader literature on welfare analysis in
the automobile industry. Studies by Xiao and Ju (2014) and Tan et al. (2019) examine
the welfare effects of consumption and fuel tax adjustments, while Xiao et al. (2017) and
Li (2018) quantify the effects of vehicle quota systems. Shen et al. (2021) compare the
welfare impacts of a consumption tax versus a tariff. A common ﬁnding across these stud-
ies is that additional taxes, vehicle registration quotas, and driving restrictions reduce ve-
hicle sales and miles driven, leading to lower greenhouse gas and air pollutant emissions.
  The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 1 provides a historical
overview of EV subsidy policies in China over the past decade and describes the data.
The empirical model and estimation strategy are detailed in section 2, while sections 3
and 4 present and discuss the estimation and counterfactual results. Finally, section 5
concludes.


1. POLICY BACKGROUND AND DATA
1.1. EV Subsidy Policies in China
Over the past two decades, China’s automobile market has experienced rapid growth.
According to the National Bureau of Statistics of China, the number of household ve-
hicles nationwide reached 225.09 million in 2019, 23 times higher than in 2002.4

However, this growth has also led to increased urban trafﬁc congestion and environ-
mental pollution. In 2019, motor vehicle pollutant emissions totaled 16.04 million tons,
making vehicles one of the major sources of air pollution in China (MEE 2020). In
response, the Chinese government has implemented various policies, such as vehicle
license quotas (Xiao et al. 2017; Li 2018), consumption taxes (Xiao and Ju 2014),
fuel taxes (Tan et al. 2019), and one-day-per-week driving restrictions (Viard and Fu
2015). While these policies effectively limit vehicle purchases and use, they do not fully
address the issue of vehicle pollution (Muller and Mendelsohn 2009; Huo et al. 2013).
  Amid these challenges, electric vehicles (EVs), particularly battery electric vehicles
(BEVs), have gained attention for their low (PHEVs) or zero (BEVs) tailpipe emis-
sions. The emergence of EVs has spurred the electriﬁcation of the transportation sec-
tor, accelerated the transformation of the automobile industry, and offered a potential
solution to vehicle tailpipe emissions, a major source of air pollution. However, con-
sumers still have concerns about EVs, such as limited driving ranges per charge, imma-
ture technology, and relatively high prices (Hidrue et al. 2011; Egbue and Long 2012).
  To promote EV sales, the Chinese government introduced a direct purchase rebate
in 2010. The subsidy is paid to manufacturers, who pass it on by reducing vehicle prices.
Initially, the subsidy was based solely on the electric driving range. Table A1 (tables A1–
A10 are available online) shows how the electric driving range subsidy was calculated from
2010 to 2019. Before 2013, BEVs and PHEVs received subsidies of 60,000 RMB
and 50,000 RMB, respectively, which equaled about 30% of the average EV price at that
time. Over time, the subsidy threshold increased, and the subsidy amount decreased. By
the second half of 2019, only BEVs with driving ranges greater than 250 kilometers (km)
qualiﬁed for a subsidy, with amounts ranging from 18,000 RMB to 25,000 RMB de-
pending on whether the driving range exceeded 400 kilometers. These changes reﬂect
the government’s intention to phase out the subsidy over time and shift from promoting
all EVs in the early market stages to encouraging high-quality EVs, particularly those
with longer driving ranges, as the market matured.5
   In 2016, another major change to the subsidy policy was introduced: adjustment
coefﬁcients targeting technological innovations. A subsidy based solely on driving range
encourages manufacturers to increase range by simply adding more batteries. However,
this makes the car heavier, leading to less energy efﬁciency. An alternative approach is
to develop better batteries with higher energy density or reduce the weight of other car
parts to offset the weight of the additional batteries. These methods require technolog-
ical innovations in battery and car design to improve energy efﬁciency.
  To promote these innovations, the government introduced two adjustment coefﬁ-
cients to the subsidy calculation formula. The ﬁrst targets battery energy density for
BEVs only. Table A2 lists the adjustment coefﬁcients for each year. For example,

1.2. Data
To measure the welfare effects of EV subsidy policies, we compiled a unique dataset
from multiple sources. This subsection provides a detailed description of each data
source.

potential buyers of new vehicles, regardless of whether they own a vehicle or not. Ad-

2.1.2. Moment Conditions, Identiﬁcation, and GMM Estimation
One challenge in estimating the demand model (4)–(6) is that the price variable is
likely to be correlated with the time-varying unobserved product characteristics Dyjt,
as companies make pricing decisions based on consumer valuations of products in spe-
ciﬁc markets. This correlation renders the price variable endogenous. To address this
endogeneity issue, we construct a generalized method of moments (GMM) estimator
using moment conditions derived from both market-level data (aggregate moments)
and micro-level household survey data (micro moments).
  The aggregate moments are constructed by interacting instrumental variables with
the unobserved product characteristics Dyjt. The ideal instruments are variables that
are correlated with prices but uncorrelated with the unobserved product characteristics.
The ﬁrst set of instruments includes 12 Berry et al.–type instrumental variables: the
number of other vehicles produced by the same ﬁrm, the number of vehicles produced
by other ﬁrms, and the sum of own and rival product characteristics for the following
variables: FC100, PC100, log(HP/Weight), log(EDR/Weight), and Size. These Berry
et al.–type instruments capture competition intensity and differences in ﬁrm portfolios,
inﬂuencing pricing decisions (Berry and Jia 2010; Miravete et al. 2018; Barwick et al.
2021).
  The second set of instruments comprises 10 cost shifters, as used in Xiao et al.
(2017). These include the interaction between labor costs in the manufacturing sector
with ﬁve exogenous product characteristics (FC100, PC100, log(HP/Weight), log
(EDR/Weight), and Size), and the interaction between the producer price index of
the metallurgical industry and these exogenous product characteristics.17 Cost shifters
provide an exogenous source of variation in prices, helping to identify the demand
model.
  The third set of instruments includes the consumption tax rate and the purchase tax
rate.18 These taxes form part of the ﬁnal price consumers pay for a vehicle, so they are
correlated with the endogenous price variable. However, these taxes are set by the gov-
ernment through regulations and are unlikely to be correlated with unobserved product
characteristics.

The ﬁnal component of social welfare is the social externality of vehicle usage. These
externalities primarily include environmental issues arising from power generation and
tailpipe emissions (Holland et al. 2016) as well as trafﬁc congestion and accidents

4.2.2. Effects of Adjustment Coefﬁcients Targeting Technological Innovations
Next, we examine the additional welfare effects resulting from the use of adjustment
coefﬁcients targeting technological innovations. Comparing the last two columns of ta-
ble 5, we observe that these adjustments lead to a 4.97% decrease in vehicle sales
(–365,893 units). This decrease is driven by a 1.16% increase in ICEV sales (80,516 units)
and a 161.95% decrease in BEVs (–442,307 units) and a 4.12% decrease in PHEVs
(–4,102 units). Regarding consumer prices, the sales-weighted average price for all
vehicles increases by 4.7%, primarily due to the increase in the sales-weighted average
EV price. The sales-weighted average ICEV price remains largely unchanged. Thus, the
use of adjustment coefﬁcients targeting technological innovations appears to have two pri-
mary effects on market outcomes. First, it causes the vehicle market to shrink, as the ad-
justments effectively render some EVs either ineligible for subsidies or eligible for a re-
duced subsidy. Second, it reduces the competitive intensity among EVs, since fewer
EVs are eligible for subsidies, leading to higher EV prices and less consumer substitution
from ICEVs to EVs.

   Turning to welfare effects, the third column of table 6 reports the welfare effects of
using adjustment coefﬁcients targeting technological innovations, broken down by com-
ponent, under case 1. First, unlike the baseline subsidy, these adjustments beneﬁt the gov-
ernment. The marginal effect on consumer welfare is negative, as the adjustments result
in fewer vehicles sold and higher average prices. Consequently, ICEV manufacturers see
an increase in proﬁts, while EV manufacturers experience a smaller decrease in proﬁts,
resulting in a net increase in total proﬁts for manufacturers. Second, with regard to en-
vironmental externalities, the use of adjustment coefﬁcients targeting technological inno-
vations leads to a welfare gain due to lower emissions of greenhouse gases (CO2) and air
pollutants (PM2.5, SO2, and NOx). The magnitude of this effect is 1,524.51 million
RMB. This effect is the opposite of the baseline subsidy, which leads to a decrease in wel-
fare because of higher emissions. This ﬁnding demonstrates that adjustment coefﬁcients
targeting technological innovations positively impact the environment, as they encourage
ﬁrms to adopt technologies with fewer environmental externalities. This result is consis-
tent with one of the key ﬁndings of Barwick et al. (2024), who suggest that attribute-
based subsidy designs improve welfare by motivating ﬁrms to select attributes with
smaller environmental impacts. Finally, the additional adjustments contribute to a wel-
fare gain by reducing trafﬁc accidents and congestion, driven by the decrease in the total
number of vehicles on the road.
  When we combine all welfare components, our results show that the marginal effect
of using adjustment coefﬁcients targeting technological innovations on social welfare is
an increase of 37,156.53 million RMB. Given that total vehicle sales in the second half
of 2019 amounted to 1,412,348.59 million RMB, the additional welfare effects from
these adjustments represent 2.63% of total vehicle sales.
  The fourth column of table 6 presents the welfare effects of applying adjustment
coefﬁcients targeting technological innovations under case 2, where the outside choice
is assumed to be continuing to drive a ﬁve-year-old ICEV.
   Since these adjustment coefﬁcients reduce overall vehicle sales—particularly EV
sales—more households opt for the outside choice. Consequently, shifting the as-
sumption from biking/walking to driving an ICEV results in a larger positive impact
of the adjustment coefﬁcients on government fuel tax revenue and a larger negative impact
on the externalities component of social welfare, due to increased CO2 and pollutant
emissions as well as higher trafﬁc congestion and accident-related externalities.
  As a result, under case 2, the total effect of the adjustment coefﬁcients on social
welfare is a decrease of 67.39 million RMB.


4.2.3. Total Welfare Effects
The ﬁfth column of table 6 presents the total welfare effects of EV subsidies under
case 1, where the outside choice is assumed to be biking or walking. All components ex-
hibit the same directional effects as the baseline subsidy. However, the total effect on
social welfare is reduced by 90.28% in magnitude. This reduction is primarily driven

by two factors. First, the additional adjustments make some EVs ineligible for subsidies
or eligible for a reduced subsidy, improving the relative competitiveness of ICEVs and
reducing the total proﬁt loss for car manufacturers. Second, the adjustments shift EV
sales from lower-technology models to higher-technology models, which helps reduce
greenhouse gas and air pollutant emissions. Taking all components into account, the to-
tal social welfare loss due to the subsidies under case 1 amounts to 4,000.57 million
RMB, or 0.28% of total vehicle sales during the same period.
  The last column of table 6 presents the total welfare effects of EV subsidies under
case 2, where the outside choice is assumed to be continuing to drive a ﬁve-year-old
ICEV. In this case, the total welfare effect of the subsidies is a gain of 3,373.71 million
RMB, or 0.24% of total vehicle sales, which contrasts with the negative effect observed
under case 1. A key reason for this difference lies in the impact of subsidies on trafﬁc con-
gestion and accidents. Under case 1, the subsidies result in a welfare loss of 4,961.64 mil-
lion RMB from increased congestion and accidents, as some consumers shift from biking/
walking to purchasing a vehicle. In contrast, under case 2, the subsidies generate a welfare
gain of 708.4 million RMB, as they incentivize consumers to switch from driving an older
ICEV to purchasing an EV. Since, on average, ICEVs are driven 1.43 times more annu-
ally than EVs (as discussed in sec. 4.1.2), this shift reduces congestion and accidents.
   These ﬁndings underscore the importance of considering different assumptions re-
garding the transportation mode underlying the outside choice when evaluating the
welfare effects of EV subsidies. As seen in table 6, different assumptions can lead to
opposite conclusions about the overall welfare impact.
   Another key takeaway from table 6 is the signiﬁcance of including nonenvironmental
externalities—such as trafﬁc congestion and accidents—in welfare analysis. Under case 1,
omitting these factors would reverse the total welfare effect from negative to positive. Un-
der case 2, ignoring them would reduce the total welfare effect by nearly 21%. This high-
lights that excluding nonenvironmental externalities in welfare evaluations could lead to
substantially different conclusions about the effects of EV subsidies.


5. CONCLUSIONS
This study estimates the welfare effects of EV subsidies on consumer welfare, manu-
facturer proﬁts, government revenue, environmental externalities (greenhouse gas and
air pollution emissions), and nonenvironmental externalities (trafﬁc accidents and con-
gestion) using a structural demand and supply model. We decompose the effects into
two components: the baseline subsidy based on driving range and the adjustment co-
efﬁcients targeting technological innovations.
  Our results show that the subsidies primarily beneﬁt consumers, with manufac-
turers receiving a small share. This is driven by price-sensitive consumers, particularly
low-income households, who are attracted to EVs through lower prices enabled by the
subsidy. Our analysis further shows that adjustment coefﬁcients targeting technological
innovations negatively impact manufacturer proﬁts by intensifying competition among

===== [PDF 第 37 页｜印刷第 37 页] =====
                                Welfare Effects of Electric Vehicle Subsidies      Ji et al.   37

EVs but positively affect environmental externalities by reducing emissions. Finally,
our results show that it is important to consider different assumptions regarding the
transportation mode underlying the outside choice and including nonenvironmental
externalities like trafﬁc accidents and congestion in welfare analysis, as varying these
assumptions could lead to drastically different conclusions.
  Our ﬁndings have several policy implications. First, while costly, the subsidy policy
mainly beneﬁts consumers and plays a crucial role in generating demand for EVs, which
is vital for an emerging industry. Without it, the EV market in China would have grown
much more slowly. Second, even though the overall welfare effect of the subsidy is small
and can be either negative or positive depending on different modeling assumptions, this
does not diminish its value, as our analysis excludes beneﬁts like economies of scale and
accelerated technological innovations driven by increased EV demand. Third, adjustment
coefﬁcients targeting technological innovations have a positive impact on environmental
protection, suggesting that increasing their weight in the subsidy calculation could enhance
both technological progress and consumer appeal. Finally, our results highlight the impor-
tance of including externalities from trafﬁc accidents and congestion, as well as considering
alternative assumptions regarding the transportation mode underlying the outside choice,
in welfare analysis, as omitting them could lead to drastically different conclusions.
  Our welfare analysis has several caveats. First, our supply model only captures short-
run price responses from manufacturers. In reality, ﬁrms may make medium- or long-
term adjustments, such as introducing new EVs or enhancing existing features (Barwick
et al. 2024). Ignoring these product changes likely leads to a conservative estimate of
welfare effects. Second, due to data limitations, we do not account for the availability
of charging stations, which Kalthaus and Sun (2021) ﬁnd positively impacts EV adop-
tion. Feedback loops between EVs and charging infrastructure, as studied in Li et al.
(2017), suggest that our welfare results may also be conservative. Third, similar to Clin-
ton and Steinberg (2019), our analysis does not consider beneﬁts from increased pro-
duction efﬁciencies, economies of scale, or accelerated innovation in the EV supply
chain. Addressing these factors would require a more complex, dynamic model and is
left for future research.
