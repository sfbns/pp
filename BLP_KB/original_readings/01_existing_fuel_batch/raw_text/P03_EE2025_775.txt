A B S T R A C T
China’s national Corporate Average Fuel Consumption (CAFC) level has decreased significantly after imple­
menting the tightened Phase IV (2016–2020) fuel economy standard. To investigate the causal effects of this 
tightened standard, we specify a difference-in-differences (DID) model to estimate the effects of this standard on 
China’s traditional internal combustion engine vehicle (ICEV) manufacturers by employing firm-level data from 
2013 to 2020. We find that this tightened standard does have a significant causal effect on inducing these 
manufacturers to reduce their CAFC by incorporating more pure electric vehicles into their product portfolios 
and reducing average fuel consumption in their traditional ICEVs by adopting currently available fuel-efficiency 
technologies. However, this tightened standard has not induced these manufacturers to implement innovation 
activities in fuel efficiency. This paper not only provides new empirical evidence to the growing literature on the 
impacts of fuel economy standards in developing countries but also sheds light on mechanisms of the impact of 
fuel economy standards.
1. Introduction
Over the last two decades, the production and sales volumes of 
passenger vehicles in China have grown considerably, along with the 
increase in Chinese households’ incomes. Production volumes of pas­
senger vehicles in China rose from 1.26 million units in 2001 to 21.41 
million units in 2021, while sales volumes increased from 1.27 million to 
21.48 million units (CATARC and CAAM, 2002 and 2022). However, the 
rapid growth in China’s passenger vehicle market also draws concerns 
about fuel consumption and carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions from the 
large volumes of passenger vehicles on roads (IEA, 2014).
China has imposed fuel consumption standards for passenger vehi­
cles to reduce fuel consumption and CO2 emissions since 2005. Phase I 
(2005–2007) and Phase II (2008–2011) standards specified in the 
standard GB 19578–2004 are mandatory weight-based stepwise stan­
dards, requiring each weight category of passenger vehicles to meet the 
corresponding fuel consumption limit (AQSIQ and SAC, 2004). To give 
automakers more flexibility to meet fuel consumption standards, Phase 
III (2012–2015) standard (GB 27999–2011) introduces the Corporate 
Average Fuel Consumption (CAFC) scheme, setting a national CAFC 
target of 7 L/100 km by 2015 for passenger vehicles (AQSIQ and SAC, 
2011). Phase IV (2016–2020) standard (GB 27999–2014) set a more 
stringent national CAFC target of 5 L/100 km by 2020 (AQSIQ and SAC, 
2014), while Phase V (2021–2025) standard (GB 27999–2019), the 
current standard, set an ambitious national CAFC target of 4 L/100 km 
by 2025 (SMAR and SAC, 2019).
China’s national CAFC level decreased significantly after imple­
menting the tightened Phase IV (2016–2020) fuel consumption standard 
(hereafter, the tightened standard). As shown in Fig. 1, although there is 
a downward trend under the Phase III (2012–2015) standard, the na­
tional CAFC only declined 5.12 % from 7.42 L/100 km in 2012 to 7.04 
L/100 km in 2015. However, under the tightened standard, the national 
CAFC declined 20.31 % from 7.04 L/100 km in 2015 to 5.61 L/100 km 
in 2020, even though the average curb weight of passenger vehicles 
increased by 9.03 % from 1385 kg to 1510 kg during the same period.
Although a significant downtrend in the national CAFC is coupled 
with the tightened standard, a formal causal analysis is required to 
attribute the decrease in the national CAFC to the tightened standard. 
Moreover, if the tightened standard does induce China’s automakers to 
reduce their CAFC, it is crucial to investigate how it affects automakers’ 
product structure and technology changes, which will help us evaluate 
the benefits and costs of the tightened standard.
This paper analyzes how the tightened standard affects product 
structure and technology changes in China’s traditional internal

turers). Employing firm-level data from 2013 to 2020, we specify a 
difference-in-differences (DID) model to estimate the causal effects of 
the tightened standard on manufacturers’ compliance behaviors. Given 
that different manufacturers may experience varying treatment in­
tensities in compliance with the tightened standard, depending on how 
close they are to achieving the tightened standard, we identify a 
continuous treatment variable to measure the stringency of the tight­
ened standard and empirically investigate whether it has induced 
manufacturers to relax the standard by incorporating new energy ve­
hicles (NEVs)1 into their product portfolios. Moreover, we explore how 
the tightened standard affects the manufacturers’ product structure in 
NEVs and technology changes.
We find that the tightened standard does have a significant causal 
effect in inducing manufacturers to reduce their CAFC. On average, 
manufacturers reduce their CAFC by 0.818 L/100 km when facing 10 % 
stricter regulations on fuel consumption rates (FCRs). This reduction is 
mainly achieved by introducing pure electric vehicles (PEVs) into their 
product portfolios and reducing average fuel consumption in traditional 
ICEVs2 by adopting currently available fuel-efficiency technologies. 
However, the tightened standard does not induce manufacturers to 
implement innovation activities in fuel efficiency.
This paper contributes to the existing literature in three aspects. 
First, it provides new empirical evidence to the growing literature on the 
impacts of fuel economy standards in developing countries, particularly 
China. Most of the previous studies on the impacts of fuel economy 
standards focus on two developed countries like the United States 
(Dowlatabadi et al., 1996; Kleit, 2004; Austin and Dinan, 2005; Jacob­
sen, 2013; Davis and Knittel, 2019; Greene et al., 2020; Leard et al., 
2023) and Japan (Kaneko, 2019; Konishi and Managi, 2020) that have 
long histories with fuel economy standards. However, fuel economy 
regulations have only recently been introduced to developing countries 
such as China and India (Anderson and Sallee, 2016), resulting in rela­
tively limited studies on their effects in these regions. Recently, several 
studies have emerged investigating the effects of fuel economy regula­
tions in China. Oliver et al. (2009) assess the impacts of China’s 
mandatory Phase I (2005–2007) and Phase II (2008–2011) standards on 
fuel economy improvements, technological changes, market composi­
tion shifts, and overall fuel savings. Hao et al. (2016) evaluate the 
impact of China’s stepwise fuel economy targets on automakers’ light­
weighting strategies, while Wu et al. (2024) address the effect of China’s 
stepwise weight-based fuel economy targets on bunching effects in 
weight distribution. Wu et al. (2021) estimate the trade-offs between 
fuel consumption and vehicle attributes in China. However, these 
studies either only address the impacts of China’s mandatory Phase I and 
Phase II standards or do not use causal analysis to explore the effects of 
China’s flexible CAFC standards on automakers’ behaviors. To our 
knowledge, this paper is the first to employ a DID design to investigate 
the causal effects of the flexible CAFC standards on automakers’ be­
haviors in China. Since China’s CAFC standards provide automakers 
with more options to achieve their targets, this paper adds new empir­
ical evidence on the impacts of China’s CAFC standards and sheds light 
on the impacts of similar standards in other developing countries.
Second, this paper adds empirical evidence of how fuel economy 
regulations induce technology changes in fuel efficiency by employing 
data from both vehicle attributes and fuel-efficiency patents. Previous 
studies use either patent counts (Berry et al., 1996; Crabb and Johnson, 
2010) or vehicle attributes (Knittel, 2011; Klier and Linn, 2012; Klier 
and Linn, 2016; Kiso, 2019) to measure technology changes primarily 
induced by the US Corporate Average Fuel Economy (CAFE) standards, 
but results are not consistent in these studies probably due to different 
measurements in technology changes on fuel efficiency. Our study ad­
dresses this issue by distinguishing between technology adoption and 
technology innovation to document the effects of China’s CAFC stan­
dards on technology changes. We find that tightening China’s CAFC 
standards can induce technology adoption rather than technology 
innovation under the same sample framework and model design. This 
finding highlights the importance of distinguishing technology adoption 
and technology innovation when investigating induced technology 
changes by fuel economy regulations.
Third, this paper sheds light on empirical evidence of how auto­
makers in China use several compliance strategies to relax regulatory 
constraints and reduce their overall compliance costs when facing 
tightening CAFC standards. Literature has theoretically shown that 
constrained automakers equate the marginal cost of improving fuel 
economy among different compliance methods (Anderson and Sallee, 
2011). Although China’s CAFC standards provide automakers with 
flexible methods to achieve their CAFC targets for their passenger 
vehicle fleet, there is still a lack of empirical studies on how China’s 
automakers reduce their compliance costs through different compliance 
strategies. Our study fills this gap by providing evidence that auto­
makers in China incorporated more PEVs into their product portfolios to 
relax regulatory constraints. Furthermore, the results of our event study 
also imply that automakers in China have incentives to buy credits from 
markets to offset their CAFC debts under the dual-credit policy associ­
ated with China’s CAFC standards,3 which may further reduce their 
overall compliance costs.
The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 briefly reviews 
fuel economy regulations for passenger vehicles in China. Section 3
develops the DID model for our empirical analysis. The data are pre­
sented in section 4. Section 5 reports the baseline results and discusses 
the validity of the design and robustness. Section 6 further explores the 
mechanisms of the impact of the tightened standard. Finally, section 7
concludes.

In 2004, China introduced its first fuel economy regulation for pas­
senger vehicles specified in the national standard GB 19578–2004 
(AQSIQ and SAC, 2004). The standard divides passenger vehicles into 16 
weight classes and establishes two-phase FCR limits for each weight- 
class passenger vehicle. Phases I and II FCR limits applied to the new- 
certificated passenger vehicles in July 2005 and January 2008, respec­
tively. However, current in-production passenger vehicles would be 
regulated by these two-phase FCR limits one year later than the new- 
certificated ones. To eliminate outdated ICEV models with high fuel 
consumption, Phases I and II FCR limits are mandatory, requiring each 
new ICEV model to meet the corresponding FCR limit based on its curb 
weight; otherwise, the ICEV model will not be allowed to sell on the 
market.
China issued Phase III fuel consumption standard effective from 2012 
to 2015 in 2011, introducing the CAFC scheme specified in the standard 
GB 27999–2011 (AQSIQ and SAC, 2011). Phase III standard proposed an 
average FCR target of 7 L/100 km for newly produced passenger vehi­
cles by 2015. Similar to the US CAFE standard, China’s CAFC standard 
employs each automaker instead of each vehicle model as an evaluated 
unit for fuel consumption, requiring just a vehicle fleet from an auto­
maker to meet its CAFC target instead of each passenger vehicle model 
within the automaker to meet the corresponding target for each model. 
Hence, the CAFC scheme gives automakers more flexibility in achieving 
FCR targets. According to the CAFC scheme, the targeted CAFC (TCAFC)

The tightened Phase IV fuel consumption standard (2016–2020)

producing NEVs or buying NEV credits from the market. However, the 
dual-credit policy has the potential to impede manufacturers’ efforts in 
reducing their CAFC from ICEVs, considering that manufacturers may 
rely on NEV credits bought from the market instead of reductions in 
their CAFC from ICEVs to offset their CAFC debts.
Several enforcement arrangements may provide incentives for the 
manufacturers to achieve the tightened standard. Although there is no 
direct financial penalty, the manufacturers who fail to offset their CAFC 
debts for a given year could be listed as dishonest companies, forced to 
suspend announcements for ICEV new models that exceed the FCRs 
specified in the tightened standard, or forced to suspend production for 
existing ICEV models with higher FCRs (MIIT et al., 2017), all of which 
may impose significant costs to manufacturers. Moreover, perceiving 
that currently implementing FCR targets for different curb weight cat­
egories may become mandatory FCR limits shortly,5 manufacturers have 
incentives to try to meet the FCR targets listed in the tightened standard; 
otherwise, non-compliance ICEV model will be prohibited from selling 
on the market in the future.
Although the above enforcement arrangements may induce manu­
facturers to comply with the tightened standard, unlike the mandatory 
Phases I and II FCR limits, the tightened standard under the CAFC 
scheme is essentially a flexible standard that gives manufacturers flex­
ibility in deciding on whether to and how to achieve their CAFC targets 
according to their own discretion. Since the CAFC scheme employs each 
manufacturer instead of each vehicle model as an evaluated unit for fuel 
consumption, each passenger vehicle model within a manufacturer does 
not need to meet the corresponding target for each model. Moreover, 
even if each manufacturer will be evaluated yearly by its CAFC target 
based on its vehicle fleet, it could still make its own decisions on whether 
to and how to achieve its CAFC target by evaluating compliance and 
violation costs. If the manufacturer perceives that the compliance costs 
are much higher than the violation costs, it has incentives to violate the 
standard and just bear the violation costs; otherwise, it has incentives to 
comply with the standard. When the manufacturer decides to achieve its 
CAFC target, it could also make its own decisions on how to comply with 
the CAFC target by comparing compliance costs among several methods, 
such as reducing its own CAFC, incorporating NEVs into its vehicle fleet, 
and buying CAFC and NEV credits from the market.
3. Model
To identify the causal effects of the tightened standard on manu­
facturers’ compliance behaviors, we specify the following difference-in- 
differences (DID) model: 
Yit = δTreat intensityi × Postt + γi + τt + εit,
(3) 
where Yit is the outcome of interest for manufacturer i at year t. 
Treat_intensityi is a continuous variable representing the treatment in­
tensity of the tightened standard faced by manufacturer i. Postt is a 
dummy variable that equals one from 2016 onwards when the tightened 
standard started to be effective. γi is the fixed effects for manufacturers, 
τt is the fixed effects for years, and εit is the error term.6 Our primary 
interest parameter δ captures the average changes in outcomes for 
manufacturers faced with higher treatment intensity from the tightened 
standard relative to those faced with lower treatment intensity.
Our identification strategy primarily relies on cross-sectional varia­
tion in the stringency of the tightened standard. Although the tightened 
standard affects the entire passenger vehicle industry in China, different 
manufacturers may experience varying treatment intensities in compli­
ance with the tightened standard, depending on how close they are to 
achieving the tightened standard. Following Klier and Linn (2016), we 
define the treatment intensity as the average logarithm differences of a 
manufacturer’s pre-treatment CAFC from 2013 to 2015, pre-treatment 
years before the tightened standard, to this manufacturer’s corre­

our treatment specification, we specify an alternative discrete treatment

cannot meet the less stringent Phase III standard before implementing 
the tightened standard, it has a high probability of being affected by the 
more stringent Phase IV standard.

NEVs reveal two channels for manufacturers to reduce their CAFC.7 To 
comply with the tightened standard, manufacturers could reduce their

4. Data
We assemble the primary firm-level data set for the years 2013 to 
2020 from several sources. The CAFC and NEVs production8 are from 
the multiple years of the annual announcement of the CAFC for China’s 
passenger vehicles (2013–2015) and annual announcement of the 
credits of CAFC and NEVs for China’s passenger vehicles (2016–2020) in 
the website of Ministry of Industry and Information Technology (MIIT) 
of China.9 We obtain the production volume of different types of NEVs 
for each manufacturer between 2013 and 201910 from multiple years of 
the Energy-Saving and New Energy Vehicles Yearbook.11 Detailed at­
tributes for each ICEV model are from Chinacar’s automotive 
announcement inquiry system.12 Filed patents on fuel-efficiency tech­
nologies for each manufacturer are from the patent search and analysis 
system of the China National Intellectual Property Administration.13
We clear our data set in several steps. First, we consider only do­
mestic manufacturers in China by removing observations for importers 
of passenger vehicles and NEV automakers who exclusively produced 
NEVs before 2016. Second, to obtain attributes for new passenger 
vehicle models, we use key terms such as “passenger vehicle” and “car” 
to identify a catalog of passenger vehicles in the automotive 
announcement inquiry system. Third, we delete observations that only 
appear before or after 2016 since we want to capture the impacts of the 
tightened standard on existing domestic manufacturers. Finally, we have 
a sample with 664 observations from 90 manufacturers for analysis.

5. Results
This section presents empirical evidence of the tightened standard on 
manufacturers’ compliance behaviors. After summarizing the baseline 
DID results, we test the validity of our DID design and do a robustness 
check for our main findings.
5.1. Baseline difference-in-differences results
Table 3 reports estimates of the baseline results of the tightened 
standard for the dependent variables of the CAFC, proportion of NEVs, 
and CAFC excluding NEVs. Column 1 shows results for the CAFC, col­
umn 2 shows results for the proportion of NEVs, and column 3 shows 
results for the CAFC excluding NEVs. Column 1 indicates that the 
tightened standard does have a significant causal effect in inducing 
manufacturers to reduce their CAFC. Manufacturers reduce their CAFC 
by 0.818 L/100 km on average when facing 10 % more stringent regu­
lations under the tightened standard.
The results in column 1 of Table 3 not only give us the overall effect 
of the tightened standard but also help us explore the underlying eco­
nomic decisions for complying with the tightened standard. Given that 
the tightened standard is flexible, manufacturers will decide whether to 
comply by evaluating compliance and violation costs. If the compliance 
costs are much lower than the violation costs, the rational manufacturers 
have incentives to comply with the standard. However, if the compli­
ance costs are much higher than the violation costs, the rational man­
ufacturers have incentives to violate the standard and just bear the 
violation costs. Therefore, whether manufacturers would reduce their 
CAFC depends on the relative costs between compliance and violation, 
which is an empirical issue to be tested. The results in column 1 of 
Table 3 imply that enforcement arrangements associated with the 
tightened standard have imposed higher violation costs on manufac­
turers and provided them incentives to reduce their CAFC.
Columns 2 and 3 in Table 3 capture two channels driving manufac­
turers to reduce their CAFC. Specifically, columns 2 and 3 show that, in 
response to a 10 % increase in regulatory strictness from the tightened 
standard, manufacturers increase the proportion of NEVs in their total 
passenger vehicles by 2.849 percentage points and reduce their CAFC 
excluding NEVs by 0.568 L/100 km. Therefore, the tightened standard 
not only induces manufacturers to reduce average fuel consumption 
from their ICEVs but also induces them to incorporate more NEVs into 
their product portfolios to relax the strictness of the tightened standard. 
Although the treatment effect on the proportion of NEVs seems small at 
first glance, incorporating some NEVs into the manufacturers’ product 
portfolios significantly relaxes the stringency of the tightened standard, 
given that the treatment effect on the CAFC excluding NEVs is only 
69.367 % ((−5.677)/(−8.184)*100 %) of the one on the CAFC including 
NEVs.
The results in columns 2 and 3 of Table 3 reveal the underlying 
economic decisions to meet the tightened standard. When manufac­
turers have incentives to reduce their CAFC, they will consider mini­
mizing total compliance costs for the two currently available methods. 
The results in columns 2 and 3 imply that using only ICEVs to meet the 
tightened standard may lead to higher compliance costs, and incorpo­
rating NEVs into the manufacturers’ product portfolios can help reduce 
their total compliance costs.

Consumer preference for passenger vehicles could explain why 
manufacturers have incentives to adopt fuel-efficiency technologies, 
particularly in their medium and large ICEVs, instead of downsizing 
vehicle attributes to meet the tightened standard. Recently, along with 
increasing income for Chinese consumers, there has been a trend toward 
larger, heavier, and more functionally powerful vehicles (Zhang et al., 
2023). As a result, it is a rational choice for manufacturers not to 
downsize vehicle attributes to meet the tightened standard. However, it 
could help manufacturers not only reduce their CAFC from ICEVs but 
also increase their profits by raising the penetration rates of fuel- 
efficiency technologies in their medium and large ICEVs,21 as con­
sumers are willing to pay a premium for vehicles adopting fuel- 
efficiency technologies when these technologies are cost-effective.
Improving specific power is related to technologies for efficient en­
gines. Several commercialized prevalent technologies for efficient en­
gines have already been adopted in China’s ICEVs. These technologies 
include gasoline direct injection (GDI), turbocharging, variable valve 
timing (VVT), variable valve lift (VVL), cooled exhaust gas recirculation 
(EGR) for naturally aspirated engines, Atkinson cycle, reinforced starter, 
and friction reduction (Yang et al., 2018). Knittel (2011) briefly in­
troduces technological progress in engine technology.
Lightweighting strategies include combining lightweight design with 
numerically optimized structures and advanced lightweight materials 
(Kaluza et al., 2018). In addition to design, the prevalent lightweighting 
strategy is the application of new materials (Czerwinski, 2021). As 
materials that have the potential to substitute for conventional engi­
neering materials (e.g., steel and cast iron), lightweight materials for 
automobiles include alloys (e.g., aluminum, magnesium, and titanium 
alloys), high strength steel (HSS) family (e.g., conventional HSSs and 
advanced HSSs), composites (e.g., carbon fiber reinforced plastics), and 
advanced materials (e.g., mechanical meta-materials) (Zhang and Xu, 
2022).

Table 9 presents the estimated effects of the tightened standard on 
patent applications for different fuel-efficiency technologies. Results

supporting evidence of induced innovation from the tightened standard 
based on patent application data.
There are two possible explanations why the impacts of the tightened 
standard on technology innovation have not been reflected in fuel- 
efficiency patent counts. First, even if patents may represent innova­
tion activities to some degree, the treatment effects on patent counts 
may not be captured within several years after the tightened standard 
due to the time lag between innovation activities and patents.25 Second, 
as noted by Kiso (2019), although patents are an essential part of 
innovation, it is better to use product characteristics (e.g., vehicle at­
tributes) instead of patent counts to measure induced technological 
changes since fuel-efficiency patents themselves cannot reduce fuel 
consumption and those patents must be commercialized and adopted in 
marketed products.
However, at first glance, it seems difficult to understand the negative 
estimated coefficients of the interaction term with relatively large 
magnitudes in absolute values for patents in PEVs, hybrids, and light­
weighting. Section 2 mentions that PEVs are promoted by China with 
several preferential policies. Meanwhile, studies indicate that hybrid 
technology has the potential to play an essential role in the transition 
from ICEVs to NEVs (Zhao et al., 2016), and lightweighting is critical to 
further reducing fuel consumption from passenger vehicles (Czerwinski, 
2021). All of these factors help provide incentives to the manufacturers 
with higher innovating abilities to invest in fuel-efficiency technologies 
for PEVs, hybrids, and lightweighting, which will increase their long-run 
competitiveness. Furthermore, there is a high possibility that the man­
ufacturers facing more (less) stringent regulations under the tightened 
standard have lower (higher) innovating abilities.26 Facing more strin­
gent regulations under the tightened standard, these manufacturers 
need to allocate more resources to meet the tightened standard typically 
by adopting current commercially available fuel-efficiency technologies 
in the short run. Accordingly, negative signs of treatment effects are 
observed for all fuel-efficiency patents, particularly for PEVs, hybrids, 
and lightweighting.
7. Conclusions
This paper uses firm-level data from 2013 to 2020 to explore how 
China’s ICEV manufacturers would respond to the more stringent Phase 
IV (2016–2020) fuel consumption standard. Relying on the variation in 
the stringency of the tightened standard across manufacturers, we 
investigate the causal effects of the tightened standard on manufac­
turers’ compliance behaviors using a difference-in-differences design. 
Our estimated DID results show that the tightened standard significantly 
induced manufacturers to reduce their CAFC. Manufacturers reduced 
their CAFC on average by 0.818 L/100 km when facing 10 % stricter 
regulations under the tightened standard. These CAFC reductions were 
achieved by introducing NEVs into manufacturers’ product portfolios 
and reducing average FCRs in ICEVs. Further analysis indicates that the 
tightened standard stimulated manufacturers to incorporate more PEVs 
instead of the other NEVs into their product portfolios and induced them 
to adopt more fuel-efficiency technologies to reduce average FCRs for 
their ICEVs. However, we do not find evidence that the tightened 
standard induced manufacturers to participate in fuel-efficiency 
innovation.
Our findings have at least two policy implications. First, total social 
costs need to be evaluated for China’s CAFC regulations in favor of PEVs. 
Our results indicate that China’s CAFC regulations induced manufac­
turers to use more PEVs to relax the strictness of the tightened standard. 
However, relatively lower electricity costs for PEVs could lead to a 
rebound effect on energy consumption. Moreover, given the high fossil 
energy consumption and greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions from the 
battery, vehicle life cycle GHG emissions and fossil energy consumption 
from PEVs may be higher than those from ICEVs (Yan and Sun, 2021). 
Considering that coal is still an overwhelming power source in China, 
the benefit of mitigating CO2 emissions from PEVs cannot be guaranteed 
either (Wu et al., 2012). Second, policymakers should evaluate the ef­
fects of China’s fuel economy regulations on the induced technical 
change in fuel efficiency. Our results show that the tightened standard in 
China induced manufacturers to adopt current commercially available 
fuel-efficiency technologies to reduce their CAFC, but did not induce 
them to implement innovation activities in fuel efficiency that are crit­
ical to fulfill even stricter future fuel economy standards. Therefore, 
policymakers should consider designing optimal fuel economy standards 
that will help promote long-run innovations in fuel efficiency.
