A B S T R A C T   
The depreciation pattern of passenger vehicles is an important input parameter for economic considerations of 
buyers and other stakeholders such as financial insti-tutions. Due to their technical specifications and un­
certainties in their degradation and life expectancy, it seems plausible that electric vehicles depreciate differently 
to conventional vehicles. This paper contributes to existing research in the economics of electric vehicles by 
analyzing the depreciation of electric vehicles along several countries and segments and compares it to gasoline 
vehicles by empirical analysis of public available data of 24,000 used vehicle sales. The results show that vehicles 
have a degressive depreciation relationship over the age of the vehicle, but that elec-tric vehicles have a sub­
stantially higher depreciation of 1.16% per month (13.9% per annum) compared to gasoline vehicles with 0.87% 
per month (10.4% per annum). Consequently, research into the economics of vehicles and budgeting consid­
erations should apply a different depreciation rate for electric vehicles than for conventional vehicles. The 
resulting difference in depreciation to gasoline vehicles imply adapta-tion in economic models of insurance and 
leasing companies and gives governments the opportunity to adapt subsidies for electric vehicles to become more 
efficient.   
1. Introduction 
“E-mobility is the only way to reduce carbon dioxide emissions in 
individual mobility quickly and efficiently” 
– Herbert Diess, CEO Volkswagen Group (2021) 
Climate change represents an impetus for industries worldwide to 
change their business models to more sustainable models. This devel­
opment is of particular importance to the automotive industry because 
greenhouse gases from road transport contribute 17% of global emis­
sions (IPCC, 2014). For many years, the automotive industry has been 
subject to changes in its business model because of growing digitaliza­
tion, new forms of mobility and an increased focus on sustainability. The 
latter in particular can be seen in the increased requirement for fleet 
electrification (Mohr et al., 2016). The electrification of passenger ve­
hicles is one of the overriding challenges for the automotive industry, 
which is also magnified by increasing regulatory pressure to switch to 
low emission vehicles in many countries. Developed countries in 
particular have incentive schemes for electric vehicles (EVs) such as 
reduced parking costs or even direct subsidies on the purchase price of 
the vehicle (IEA, 2021). At the same time, many are introducing re­
strictions and levies on conventional vehicles such as city tolls or are 
planning to ban them completely (IEA, 2021). 
The regulatory support together with the increased environmental 
awareness of con-sumers have resulted in an increased number of new 
EV registrations in many developed countries. For many years, Norway 
has been the forerunner in new EV registrations with a share of 74% in 
2020 (EAFO, 2020a). More recently, however, other European countries 
have been catching up in respect of new EV registrations, for example 
Germany (13%) or the Netherlands (25%) (EAFO, 2020c,d). As one of 
the largest automotive markets worldwide, the USA currently has a very 
small proportion of just 2% of new registrations in 2020, but with a clear 
trend towards strongly increasing numbers (IEA, 2021). 
In addition to the increasing proportion of new EV registrations, 
many countries have also set ambitious targets for the decarbonization

an emission reduction target for new vehicles of 100% by 2035 and

aims to contribute 10 million EVs to this target by 2030 (BMU, 2019). 
The aim of the Netherlands is to achieve 100% zero emission vehicle 
registrations by 2030 and Norway even earlier - by 2025 (Wappelhorst, 
2021; Ministry of Transportation Norway, 2021). The USA aims to

The technical concept of an EV differs fundamentally from that of a 
conventional ve-hicles resulting in a different bill of materials (BOM) for 
the EV. Instead of a complex combustion engine, a much simpler and 
smaller electric engine propels the car. Further-more, the fuel tank is 
replaced by a heavy, much larger and more costly electric battery. 
Additionally, high-voltage power electronics and wiring are required 
(DOE, 2021). 
Compared to conventional vehicles, the different technical layout of 
EVs with a large battery and a much smaller electric engine implies 
changes to the cost structure of the EV in comparison with conventional 
vehicles. As the powertrain and engine are less complex and have less 
parts, they are generally cheaper when compared to conventional ve­
hicles. By contrast the battery makes up the largest fraction of the costs 
with roughly 40% of the total production costs (K¨onig et al., 2021). 
Experience of the mass production of EVs is roughly only a decade, 
resulting in limited but increasing knowledge on the degradation and 
life expectancy of the battery. This lack of knowledge impacts also the 
view on the depreciation of EVs. While there is a lot of data on the 
historical depreciation of conventional vehicles, little is known about 
the loss in value of EVs. 
The depreciation of a vehicle is defined as the value loss of the 
vehicle over time. To calculate the depreciation over a certain time the 
sale price of the vehicle is deducted by the current (resale) value. The 
depreciation of vehicles is an important economic in-dicator for many 
stakeholders: automotive manufacturers require the depreciation and 
life expectancy of their vehicles to estimate warranty costs. Mobility 
providers such as car rental agencies or taxi services require the 
depreciation to set their tariffs. Insurers determine their rates based on 
the expected value loss of the vehicle. Banks define the conditions of car 
credits based on the depreciation of the vehicle. Authorities and govern- 
ments require the deprecation to define subsidies, taxes and other reg­
ulatory instruments. Lastly, consumers too are keen to understand how 
the value of their investment will change over time. 
The total cost of ownership (TCO) method analyzes the total costs of 
a good such as a vehicle for a consumer over the entire period of 
ownership (Ellram, 1995). When analyzing the TCO of a vehicle in 
addition to variable costs such as fuel, the depreciation of the vehicle 
also needs to be included. Wu et al. (2015) analyze the total costs of 
ownership of EVs compared to conventional vehicles across market 
segments. They mention in their 2015-published paper that “empirical 
data were not sufficient to derive the resale value of an EV”(Wu et al. 
(2015), p.200)). 
Since Wu et al. (2015) mentioned the lack of data on EVs in 2015, the 
EV market has changed significantly. From a market-dependent 15–45 
available models in 2015, model availability has risen to over 500 
(Yang, 2016; Gersdorf et al., 2020). Electric vehicle volumes grew from 
just over half a million units in 2015 to over 6 million units in 2021 (Irle, 
2021). Ten years after the first mass production of EVs, there is now 
sufficient quantitative and qualitative data on EVs and especially on the 
used vehicle market of EVs to address the research gap on the depreci­
ation of EVs mentioned by Wu et al. (2015). 
This research presents findings on the depreciation of EVs in com­
parison with gaso-line vehicles. Quantitative data of over 24,000 used 
vehicles across several markets and segments have been analyzed. By 
applying established empirical methods depreciation rates have been 
identified and the deviation relative to that of gasoline vehicles have 
been derived. 
This paper begins with a literature review on economics of conven­
tional and electric vehicles. The following sections include the meth­
odology and an overview of the collected data. This is followed by the 
results of the data analysis including a sensitivity analysis. The paper 
finishes with a discussion of the results as well as a conclusion and im­
plications for research and other stakeholders such as insurance or 
leasing companies. 
2. Literature review 
Vehicle depreciation is relevant for many stakeholders such as 
financial institutions, gov-ernmental authorities and consumers. Con­
ventional vehicles have existed for over a cen-tury and the accounting 
profession has researched the depreciation of conventional vehicles in 
great detail. In general, the research is based on the historical data of 
vehicles such as the life expectancy and influence of various factors such 
as mileage driven, age or market developments. In his research, Peles 
(1988) analyzed the depreciation of over 34 different vehicle models in 
the United States. He compares different depreciation methods such as 
the straight line method, which is a uniformly reduction of the value of 
an asset over time, and the geometric method, which is a depreciation of 
a constant annual rate. He con-cludes that vehicles have an annual 
deprecation of 9–10% when applying the straight line method and 
21–24% when applying a geometric method. As both methods appear to 
be a good approximation for the actual depreciation he concludes that 
the true depreciation function lays somewhere between these two 
methods. 16 years later, Storchmann (2004) investigated the same issue 
of depreciation rates and patterns for vehicles in 30 countries among 54 
vehicle models. He pointed out that the geometric depreciation pattern 
is a better approximation of the true depreciation function and came to 
the conclusion that the annual depreciation for vehicles in developed 
countries is on average 31%. 
In their research, Linz et al. (2003) derived the actual year-on-year 
price increase of used vehicles in Germany by applying hedonic mea­
surement of the price. The hedonic method enables the separation of 
qualitative influencing factors such as technical progress and changes in 
consumer behavior from the actual pure price increase. For this reason, 
the researchers took monthly data on used vehicle prices, quality 
characteristics such as age, mileage driven, make and manufacturer’s 
suggested retail price (MSRP) and define a multiple regression analysis. 
The data was collected from a database of over 20,000 to 25,000 
monthly transaction price records from commercial and private sellers 
in Ger-many. The authors assume a geometric degressive depreciation 
behavior of vehicles with a semi-logarithmic correlation of the resale 
price and the age of the vehicle and a double-logarithmic correlation of 
the resale price and the MSRP. A dummy variable represents 
The influence of the make on the resale price. Use of a logarithmic 
function to derive the resale value enables the researcher to investigate 
the influence factors of each variable such as age, mileage driven or 
vehicle make on the resale price. One of the main results is that age has 
the highest impact on the resale value at roughly 15% per year. 
Gilmore and Lave (2013) were among the first researchers to 
investigate costs and depreciation of vehicles with different propulsion 
systems and especially with more efficient and environmental friendly 
propulsion systems, such as hybrid vehicles. They found that vehicles 
with better fuel consumption such as diesel or hybrid vehicles have a 
higher resale value in comparison with gasoline vehicles. Their research 
raises the question of whether the depreciation derived in the previously 
mentioned research also apply to vehicles with alternative propulsion 
systems such as EVs. 
Wu et al. (2015) compared the total cost of ownership (TCO) of EVs 
to that of conventional vehicles. The research highlights that the 
depreciation over the ownership period is the largest cost factor for the 
consumer. Hence, detailed knowledge of the depreciation is necessary 
for an accurate analysis. The authors point out that while for conven­
tional vehicles there is sufficient data to determine the resale value after 
the ownership period, for EVs “empirical data is insufficient to derive 
the resale value” (Wu et al. (2015, p.200)). Without sufficient data on 
the resale value of EVs, the researchers applied the formula developed 
by Linz et al. (2003) to approximate the resale value of EVs. 
Due to the lack of sufficient data and research on the depreciation, 
researchers inves-tigating the cost of electric vehicles often assume the 
same depreciation for EVs as for conventional vehicles (see Palmer et al. 
(2018) and Hagman et al. (2016)). By contrast Danielis et al. (2018) and

L’evay et al. (2017) both argue in their research on the TCO of electric

With the developing second hand market for used EVs, more and 
more market data is now available compared to 2015 when Wu et al. 
(2015) mentioned the insufficient empirical data. Schoettle and Sivak 
(2018) analyzed market data on resale values of used vehicles in the 
USA. They obtained sufficient data for 11 electric vehicles from the 
model years 2011–2015 to compare them with similar conventional 
vehicles. The average resale values are obtained from a database with no 
direct access to validate the raw data. A similar effort was made by Guo 
and Zhou (2019) who analyzed model years 2010–2016 of EVs in the 
USA among 11 models and 10 makes. In this case the researchers were 
able to access the raw data of 26,622 price listings from the data pro­
vider. The focus of both studies was the USA only and a time period of 6 
years. Both studies focused on the resale value without further analysis 
of the depreciation rate and behavior. They both came to a similar 
conclusion, in general, EVs loose more value over time than conven­
tional vehicles. Existing research has mostly focused on the resale value 
without analyzing the de-preciation rate and behavior as well as the 
impact of single attributes on the value. Furthermore, it is limited on 
single countries such as the USA and on a limited number of EV models 
and segments for only a time horizon of maximum 6 years. Moreover, 
the raw data of the research is not public available and its quality is hard 
to validate. 
The present research builds on the existing research in the field and 
further investigates the value retention of EVs focusing on depreciation 
rates and behavior. Over 24,000 public available data entries on used 
vehicles in several major EV markets such as the USA, Norway and 
Germany has been analyzed. The impact of different factors such as age, 
mileage and sales price on the value of the vehicle by applying a multiple 
regression analysis has been investigated. The regression function is 
derived from the previous work on vehicle depreciation by Linz et al. 
(2003). Furthermore, a sensitivity analysis on the influencing factors of 
the value retention has been conducted and compared to the deprecia­
tion behavior of gasoline vehicles. 
3. Data overview 
Five key automotive markets (see Table 1) has been selected for the 
analysis: The USA as one of the largest automotive markets in the world 
as well as Norway, Sweden, Nether-lands and Germany as the most 
rapidly growing electric vehicle markets in terms of new registrations 
(EAFO, 2020b,c,d,a; OICA, 2020). All of the selected countries have EVs. 
On the road and a second hand EV market that has been established 
for multiple years. As the loss in value of electric vehicles with con­
ventional vehicles is compared, data for electric and gasoline vehicles 
among several vehicle segments has been collected. Diesel vehicles were 
excluded because there is insufficient data for all vehicle segments and 
coun-tries. Vehicles with a battery and a combustion engine such as 
plug-in or hybrid vehicles were also excluded. 
The focus of the analysis is on EV models that have been on market 
for many years and thus have also been available in the second hand 
market for many years, as well as vehicles that have significant sales 
numbers (see Table 2). Premium automotive makes such as Tesla and 
BMW has been included as well as high volume mass-market makes such 
as Toyota and Volkswagen. The selected gasoline vehicles are in the 
same segments as the EVs and have similar vehicle characteristics in 
terms of performance and size. 
The focus is solely on online platforms as a data source to gather as 
many entries as possible. Leading regional online platforms are used, e. 
g., autoscout24.com for Germany and the Netherlands, bytbil.com for 
Sweden, finn.no for Norway and edmunds.com for the USA. Offers from 
commercial dealers as well as from private sellers are included. To 
obtain a large amount of data records, a Python script has been devel­
oped which automatically gathers the required vehicle data from the 
websites as, in general, the website interface (API) is not publicly 
available. The Python script collects data on the vehicle make, model, 
age, mileage, propulsion type, location, vendor type, performance, offer 
number and time as well as the price data. 
The price on the website is in general the seller’s offer price and not 
the actual transac-tion price of the deal. The transaction price might be 
marginal lower as the tools estimate usually the expected selling price 
for the seller and reduces the price automatically if the. 
price is to high. Hence, the deviation for used cars on the online 
platforms between the offered price and actual transaction price are 
marginal. Furthermore, this effect is valid for all vehicle types. Hence, as 
the aim of the research is to compare vehicles of different propulsion 
types and analyze the deviation in depreciation rates, the effect is not 
relevant for this research. A total of over 40,000 vehicle entries from the 
years 2020 and 2021 are collected. Afterwards, the data is cleaned up for 
entries with insufficient and inaccu-rate data and vehicles with high 
deviations in price and mileage as well as any damaged vehicles are 
excluded. Furthermore, only vehicles with a maximum age of ten years 
are considered as there is insufficient data for older vehicles. This limits 
the vehicle entries available for the analysis to 24,325. 
In general, the manufacturer’s suggested retail price (MSRP) of the 
vehicles is not available on the online platforms. For this reason, the 
exact vehicle model is collected from the online platforms and the MSRP 
from other data sources. For Germany the data source is the Allgemeiner 
Deutscher Automobil-Club e.V. (ADAC), for the USA the website aut 
oblog.com and for Norway the data from the Norwegian tax adminis­
tration is used (norw.: Skatteetaten). For the remaining countries the 
MSRP is collected from the automotive manufacturers website partly by 
using the web-archive function. The vehicle segments are assigned ac­
cording to the classification of the European Union (EC, 2002).

4. Methodology 
The data set with over 24,000 records of used electric and gasoline 
vehicles makes it possible to investigate the influence of specific vehicle 
characteristics on the value of the vehicle. The research objective is to 
obtain the depreciation of EVs and compare it with that of gasoline 
vehicles. Thus, the specific focus is on the age variable of the vehicle. In a 
first step, the aim is to understand the influence of predictor variables on 
the value of the vehicle. 
A regression analysis is a suitable method for investigating the 
relationship between a variable such as the value of a vehicle and its 
influencing factors such as age or mileage as the age has a significant 
impact on the value of the vehicle. In empirical research this method is 
widely used to investigate the relationship of a target variable with 
indepen-dent predictor variables (Chatterjee and Simonoff, 2013). The 
purpose of a least squares regression is to find coefficients in a function 
that minimize the sum of squared errors. The regression analysis applied 
in this study is based on the equation developed by Linz et al. (2003) for 
their analysis of the price development of used vehicles in Germany. 
For the case of the value of a vehicle it is assumed that not only have 
one predictor variable such as the age, but several predictor variables 
such as the mileage, MSRP and vehicle make. In the case of multiple

Due to the presence of multiple variables, the function of a multiple 
linear regression describes a hyperplane and can only be applied if 
specific requirements are fulfilled. In general, the target variable of the 
multiple linear regression needs to be defined by a linear combination of 
the predictor variables. Thus, this method can be used for the analysis of 
the vehicle depreciation only if a depreciation of the vehicle by a fixed 
amount per year or percentage in value per year is assumed. From the 
previous research of Storchmann (2004) and the analysis of the data in 
the previous section (see Fig. 3), it is known that this assumption holds 
true for the observed vehicle data. 
When choosing age, mileage and MSRP as the predictor variables of 
the target variable

Ri = β0 + β1 ⋅ Mi + β2 ⋅ Ai + β3 ⋅ Si + εi                                            
(1) 
where R is the (resale) value of vehicle i, M is the total mileage driven of

During the analysis of the data a logarithmic relationship of the value 
of the vehicle and the age can be noted (see Fig. 3). This relationship 
needs to be transferred to the equation with a logarithmic trans­

that a double-logarithmic relationship between the resale value of the 
vehicle and the MSRP exists which brings me to a logarithmic trans­
formation of the MSRP in the equation analogous to Linz et al. (2003). 
In the previous section it can be observed that in the data a corre­

Fig. 2). This multicollinearity should be eliminated to avoid errors in the 
regression analysis (Olive, 2017). Hence, Linz et al. (2003) transfer the 
variable to a mileage value per month to avoid a correlation within the 
function. A higher relative mileage per month does not necessarily mean 
a higher total age of the vehicle and thus, the correlation of the two 
predictor variables is eliminated.

Another assumed influencing factor on the vehicle value is the 
vehicle make. For example, do vehicles from some premium automotive 
companies such as Audi hold the value better compared to mass-market

have the attribute of a single make, a so called dummy variable is 
introduced analogous to Linz et al. (2003). This dummy variable can 
either be “true” (=1) or “not true” (=0) depending on whether the 
requirement is fulfilled. As a vehicle 
can only have the characteristic to be from one make this means that 
if one dummy variable of a make is “true” all other dummy variables of 
the other makes have to be “not true”. To avoid this multicollinearity 
BMW is chosen as reference make which leads to a total of 8 dummy 
variables for the observed 9 automotive makes.

The conducted regression analysis gives us the coefficients of the 
predictor variables. These coefficients for the predictor variables age, 
mileage per month, MSRP and vehicle make explain how much a change 
in a variable e.g., age influences the value of a vehicle. For the analysis of 
the depreciation it is especially interesting to understand the influence 
of age on the vehicle value. For this reason, a typical reference vehicle is 
selected as a fixed value for the remaining variables is needed to analyze 
the influence of only one of them. The reference vehicle is based on an 
approximate average value of the variables from the data set. This means 
an MSRP of EUR 40,000 and a mileage per month of 1500 km. Both 
values are validated by similar values for the average MSRP and mileage 
per month in Germany collected by Deutsche Automobil Treuhand 
GmbH (2020). BMW is selected as a reference vehicle make to set all 
dummy variables to 0. For the analysis of the MSRP and mileage per 
month, a reference age of 36 months is set. In all analyzed countries 
some sort of subsidy on the MSRP of EVs exists. Therefore, the value of 
EVs is normalized to that of gasoline vehicles which do not receive a 
subsidy when new (age = 0).

The results show that the coefficients β0, β1, β2 and β3 are significant 
by a value of > 99%. Therefore, the H0 hypothesis which implies that all 
predictor variables have no impact on the target variable ln(R) has to be 
rejected. Thus, the corresponding H1 hypothesis has to be confirmed. 
Additionally, the results of the regression show that the variables in the 
regression function have a good regression fit which is indicated by the 
high R2 value of 0.902 and 0.925. 
The results of the regression analysis by country (Germany, USA, 
Sweden, Nether-lands, Norway) can be found in the Table 4 and will be 
discussed in the graphical representation of the findings in a later ab­
stract of this section. 
The interpretation of the findings of the regression analysis gives the 
depreciation of the vehicle based on age, mileage per month and MSRP 
which are discussed separately below. 
Due to the logarithmic transformation, the depreciation of the ve­
hicles as an age variable is defined by eβ2 −1 ≈β2. As for the numerical 
values of β2 the approximation gives an error per month of roughly 
0.5%, the exact formula is used. Thus, the annual depreciation of the 
vehicle is calculated based on age by applying the equation eβ2⋅12. This 
results in an age-dependent annual geometric depreciation for EVs of 
13.1% and for gasoline vehicles of 9.9% excluding the impact of the 
other predictor variables such as MSRP or relative mileage. For the co­
efficient β1 for the relative mileage per month, an influence on the value 
of the vehicle of −0.6% per 100 km per month for EVs and −0.2% per 
100 km per month for gasoline vehicles are observed. The third coeffi­
cient β3 defines the influence of the MSRP of the vehicle. A double 
logarithmic relationship between the value of the vehicle and the MSRP 
is observed. Thus, the coefficients β3 are interpeted as elasticity of the 
price. The results show that for EVs a 1% higher MSRP is translated to a 
0.74% higher value of the vehicle. On the other hand, it can be seen that 
for gasoline vehicles this value is marginally lower with a 0.71% higher 
value of the vehicle with a 1% higher MSRP. 
In addition to the numerical values binary dummy variables δ1, …, δ8 
are implemented in the function. The dummy variables distinguish be­
tween different vehicle makes in the data set and their influence on the 
value of the vehicle. The vehicle make BMW has been selected as a 
reference make for the analysis. It can be observed that almost all other 
vehicle makes have a more negative impact on the value of the vehicle 
compared to the reference make BMW. For example the vehicle make 
Renault has a coefficient of −0.26 
which translates as 23% (EUR -5000) less value that a reference 
vehicle with an MSRP of EUR 40,000, a relative mileage of 1500 km per 
month and an age of 36 months. Tesla is the only vehicle make which 
has a positive impact on the value of the vehicle compared to the 
reference make BMW with a coefficient of +0.40. This means for the 
above mentioned reference vehicle a 50% (EUR +10,000) higher value 
of the vehicle compared to a BMW with the same characteristics.

After the analysis of the single coefficients of the regression results, a 
graphical represen-tation of the results is shown in the following. As a 
multiple linear regression defines a hyperplane, the predictor variables 
are shown in separate graphs for better understand-ing. In the following 
diagrams the relationship of each of the three numerical predictor var­
iables relative mileage, age and MSRP with the value of the vehicle is 
shown. As the variables are described separately and a fixed value for 
the other variables is selected, the graphical representation is only 
theoretical. 
Fig. 4 is the graphical representation of the change in value of a 
reference vehicle relative to its age. The MSRP of the reference vehicle is 
set to EUR 40,000, the relative mileage per month to 1500 km and the

by the MSRP to obtain the relative value. It can be noted that even at 
point zero, the EV already has a value less than 100%, i.e., 88%. This 
observation can be explained by the definition of the MSRP in the data. 
The manufacturer’s suggested retail price excludes the local subsidies 
which exist in the analyzed countries. To include subsidies in the vehicle 
prices a historical analysis of the development of subsidies over the last 
ten years in the five selected countries for different vehicle segments 
needs to be done as actual price data is not publicly available. As the 
main objective is the comparison of EVs to gasoline vehicles and this 
phenomenon also applies to the data set of gasoline vehicles, a complex 
analysis of historical real prices is neglected and a simple adaption of the 
data is applied. For this reason, the value of the EV is set at the starting 
point to the same value as for the gasoline vehicle. Afterwards, the 
depreciation is applied on the plot (dashed blue line). For this normal­
ized depreciation line it can be observed that EVs have a higher depre­
ciation compared to gasoline vehicles. Based on the regression function, 
it can be seen that a gasoline vehicle with the reference vehicle speci­
fications has a value of 56% after 5 years compared to an EV which has a 
value of only 47%. 
Furthermore, a degressive development of the value is observed. For 
the reference vehicle characteristics, a monthly depreciation for EVs of 
1.16% (13.9% per annum) and for gasoline vehicles of 0.87% (10.4% 
per annum) can be seen.

6. Discussion 
Existing research on economics of vehicles is grounding the depre­
ciation of EVs on as-sumptions and either assumes the same depreciation 
as gasoline vehicles or a delta to that of gasoline vehicles (see Wu et al. 
(2015) and Danielis et al. (2018)). Recent empir-ical research on EVs 
value development focuses on the resale value without analysis of 
depreciation rates, behavior and influencing factors. Furthermore, the 
research has often a limited scope on only a few countries, segments or 
models and is based on non-public data (see Guo and Zhou (2019) and 
Schoettle and Sivak (2018)). 
In this research the aim is to focus on the depreciation rate, behavior 
and influencing factors for electric vehicles and compare them to gas­
oline vehicles. The scope of the analysis is wider and includes 5 auto­
motive markets and 5 vehicle segments with a total of 19 vehicle models. 
The data set is from a public available source and includes over 24,000 
data records over a time horizon of 10 years. The increased scope of the 
research enables additional insights on electric vehicle adaptation, 
economics and depreciation compared to existing research. 
The data of used vehicles confirms the findings of Storchmann 
(2004) that vehicles tend to have a geometric degressive depreciation 
over time. The conducted regression analysis confirms the research 
conducted by Linz et al. (2003) on vehicle depreciation. The vehicle age, 
mileage, MSRP and make have a significant impact on the value of the 
electric vehicle. Age has the highest impact on the value of EVs with 
−1.17% and is followed by the relative mileage with −0.6% per 100 km 
per month. The MSRP is described as price elasticity in the analysis and 
results in a 0.74% higher value of the electric vehicle for a 1% higher 
MSRP. Another result of the regression analysis is the different influence 
of the vehicle make on the vehicle value. Tesla is the only vehicle make 
which increases the value of the EV compared to the reference vehicle 
make, BMW. 
The depreciation can be aggregated to an annual depreciation rate 
for EVs of 13.9% and for gasoline vehicles of 10.4%. The obtained values 
confirm existing research in this field with a similar depreciation. 
Mueller et al. (2017) obtain a depreciation of 12.5%–15% per annum 
and Morrison et al. (2018) a 12.4% depreciation per annum for EVs. Linz 
et al. (2003) indicate a result of 11.6% per annum for the depreciation 
rate. Breetz and Salon (2018) use in their analysis a depreciation rate of 
11% for gasoline vehicles and 17% for EVs based on an online database 
confirming the observed difference between the two propulsion types in 
this analysis. The observed delta between the two vehicle types could 
have several reasons which might include one of the following (see also 
Breetz and Salon (2018)):  
• less current demand for EVs on the market because consumers have 
different vehicle requirements, 
• insufficient knowledge concerning the degradation and life expec­
tancy of the battery and other parts of the EV,  
• high rate of technological advancements of EVs,  
• dynamic market with new makes and models introduced regularly. 
The higher depreciation and the underlying reasons are typical for 
nascent technologies. As Danielis et al. (2018) mentions in his research 
this circumstance might change in the future. Technological advance­
ments and the growing market might be the reason that in the near 
future the delta in depreciation between EVs and gasoline vehicles might 
decrease or even disappear. 
The analysis on a country level shows that the delta between EVs and 
gasoline vehicles occurs in all countries, but in some countries the 
depreciation and the delta between the two propulsion types is higher. 
This might be explained by the different dynamics of the national 
automotive markets. The US market has for example a high share of 
vehicles financed through short-term leasing (30% of new vehicles) 
which might stimulate higher depreciation compared to other countries 
such as Germany with a lower leasing share (22%) (Deutsche Automobil 
Treuhand GmbH, 2020; Zabritski, 2021). 
The conducted sensitivity analysis on the variables relative mileage 
and MSRP in-vestigates the influence of these variables on the value of 
the vehicle. It shows that all variables have an influence on the value of 
the vehicle when varying the variables by a realistic extend. The relative 
mileage and MSRP of the vehicle have a significant lower influence on 
the vehicle value compared to the age. Car buyers might see age as the

The MSRP of the vehicle has a significant lower impact on the value.

The conducted research has two main limitations which should be 
acknowledged. Firstly, for the original sale price of the used vehicles the 
MSRP of the vehicle type is used, which does neglect potential extra 
equipment of the car and subsidies. Due to the fact that there are over 
24,000 data records from 5 countries over a time period of 10 years it is 
very complex to get the exact subsidy and original sale price of each ve-

value to that of the gasoline vehicle, but could not been completely 
eliminated. Secondly, the analysis of the depreciation of passenger ve­
hicles is a snapshot of the current development. It can be assumed that 
the depreciation might change in the future (see Danielis et al. (2018)). 
Therefore, a similar analysis after some years would give additional 
insight on the changes in the depreciation. 
7. Conclusion and implications 
Literature on the economics of EVs lack data on the depreciation and 
researchers base their depreciation on assumptions (see Wu et al. (2015) 
and Danielis et al. (2018)). Re- cent research on value development of 
EVs focus mainly on the resale value and not on depreciation behavior 
and have a limited scope (see Guo and Zhou (2019) and Schoettle and 
Sivak (2018)). This research addresses this gap and provides a broader 
and empirical approach on the depreciation of EVs compared to gasoline 
vehicles. With a maturing EV market more sufficient data on EVs and 
their value development is public available to conduct this analysis. 
The obtained data on depreciation and the delta to gasoline vehicle is 
typical. Nascent technologies such as EVs tend to have a higher depre­
ciation due to e.g., technological advancements and other factors dis­
cussed in the previous chapter. With a maturing and growing market for 
EVs in the next years this circumstance might change and the depreci­
ation might come closer to that of gasoline vehicles. 
The results of this research have implications for research but also 
many other stake-holders. The developed equation (3) and also the re­
sults for the annual depreciation enables a more accurate and specific 
application of the depreciation for electric and gasoline ve-hicles. It 
enables a differentiation between the two propulsion types, but also 
between country and includes vehicle characteristics such as mileage 
and make. 
The results on the depreciation can be applied in research on trans­
portation policies and vehicle economics. The results can change TCO 
analysis and evaluations of different propulsion systems. The more ac­
curate and specific depreciation gives governments the opportunity to 
tailor subsidies for EVs so that they become more effective and efficient. 
Authorities can adapt tax benefits and tax depreciation rates of different 
vehicle types so that it differentiate between gasoline and electric ve­
hicles. Mobility provider such as rental companies can adapt their tariff 
according to the different depreciation rates as well as insurance com­
panies. Banks and leasing companies can use the more accurate depre­
ciation to tailor their contracts for vehicles to become more profitable. 
Automotive manufacturer can use the depreciation delta to implement 
approaches to reduce the delta 
Through measures such as an extended guarantee on EVs. Lastly, also 
consumers benefit from the more accurate and specific depreciation 
from this research. This provides to consumers more transparency on the 
costs associated with a vehicle and gives them a clearer picture on the 
value at a certain point in time. 
This research leaves also room for further research on the depreci­
ation and economics of EVs. With a growing EV market more data will 
be available in the near future to extend the research. Furthermore, the 
exact reasons behind the delta of EVs and gasoline vehicle can be 
investigated. This could help automotive manufacturer as well as gov­
ernments to address the reasons and enhance the value of the vehicles. 
Research on the degradation of the electric vehicle battery and on the 
life expectancy of the EV and its parts could improve the understanding 
of the value drivers of the EV and enhance research in vehicle 
economics.
